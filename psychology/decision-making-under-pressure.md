# Decision-Making Under Pressure

## Purpose

Make consequential choices arise from limited information, competing values, time pressure, identity, emotion, and perceived options rather than plot convenience.

## Principle

Characters choose from the options they can perceive, tolerate, and justify—not from every objectively available option.

## Mechanism

```text
problem perceived
→ options generated
→ options filtered by knowledge, identity, fear, values, and time
→ costs weighted
→ uncertainty tolerated or avoided
→ choice made
→ consequence updates future judgment
```

## Decision variables

Define:

- information available;
- information missing or misread;
- time available;
- options perceived;
- options objectively available but psychologically inaccessible;
- immediate reward;
- delayed cost;
- value conflict;
- identity stake;
- relationship stake;
- social audience;
- prior strategy and whether it succeeded;
- threshold that forces action.

## Pressure effects

Pressure can:

- narrow attention;
- privilege immediate relief;
- increase reliance on habit;
- reduce moral complexity;
- amplify loss aversion;
- make familiar bad options feel safer than unfamiliar good ones;
- create action bias or paralysis;
- shift trust toward authority, intimacy, or private intuition;
- distort probability estimates;
- make irreversible action attractive because it ends uncertainty.

Do not portray pressure as automatic stupidity. Expertise, rehearsal, and role identity can improve performance while still creating blind spots.

## Failure modes and repair

### Plot-required stupidity

A character ignores an obvious option without cause.

**Repair:** remove the option, conceal it, make it costly, make it identity-incompatible, or establish why the character distrusts it.

### Perfect optimization

The character consistently selects the objectively best strategy.

**Repair:** filter choices through emotion, incomplete knowledge, values, history, and social risk.

### False dilemma without enforcement

The story claims only two options exist when ordinary alternatives remain open.

**Repair:** establish time, institutional, relational, spatial, legal, moral, or informational constraints.

### Sudden reversal

A character changes course without sufficient new evidence, cost, or value conflict.

**Repair:** identify the threshold crossed and dramatize the evidence or sacrifice that made reversal possible.

### Choice without consequence

The decision changes nothing durable.

**Repair:** alter knowledge, power, trust, danger, commitment, identity, resources, or future possibility.

## Diagnostic questions

- Why does this option feel available while another does not?
- What is the character optimizing for: safety, belonging, control, morality, relief, status, or certainty?
- What cost are they underweighting?
- What prior experience is being overgeneralized?
- Who influences the choice merely by being present?
- What new consequence changes future decisions?

## Acceptance test

The choice passes when it is locally rational from the character's perspective, psychologically constrained, materially consequential, and capable of being traced to established appraisal, values, coping, and relationships.
