# Identity, Shame, and Guilt

## Purpose

Design identity threat, concealment, self-judgment, and repair as specific dramatic forces.

## Core distinctions

- **Identity:** the model of who the character believes they are, must be, or must never become.
- **Shame:** threat to the self as exposed, defective, weak, contaminated, ridiculous, unworthy, or outside belonging.
- **Guilt:** judgment that a behaviour, choice, or omission violated a value or harmed someone.

Shame tends toward concealment, attack, withdrawal, performance, or self-erasure. Guilt can support repair, confession, restitution, denial, or displacement.

## Mechanism

```text
identity claim
→ event contradicts claim
→ exposure risk assessed
→ shame or guilt activated
→ protection, concealment, repair, or aggression
→ consequence changes self-concept or hardens defence
```

## Identity construction

Define:

- the identity the character performs publicly;
- the identity they privately fear;
- the evidence they use to support each;
- whose judgment matters most;
- what behaviour would expose contradiction;
- what they would sacrifice to preserve the preferred identity;
- what truth could force revision.

## Behavioural expression

Identity threat may appear through:

- correction of minor details;
- controlling how an event is narrated;
- refusing help;
- attacking the witness rather than the claim;
- over-performance of competence, toughness, goodness, innocence, loyalty, or belonging;
- humour that pre-empts humiliation;
- confession with strategic omissions;
- restitution without apology;
- avoidance of mirrors, records, places, people, or rituals associated with the act;
- rage at being pitied.

## Failure modes and repair

### Shame as generic sadness

The character simply looks down or cries.

**Repair:** define the feared social meaning and the behaviour used to prevent exposure.

### Guilt without violated value

The character feels guilty because the plot expects remorse.

**Repair:** specify the value, harm, responsibility estimate, and possible repair.

### Identity stated instead of tested

Dialogue tells us who the character is.

**Repair:** place the identity claim under pressure and force a costly choice.

### Public and private selves are identical

The character has no audience-dependent behaviour.

**Repair:** define who receives performance, honesty, contempt, fear, or dependence.

### Easy absolution

A confession erases consequence.

**Repair:** separate acknowledgment, forgiveness, restitution, trust, and self-forgiveness. They do not occur automatically or together.

## Scene application

```text
IDENTITY CLAIM:
FEARED IDENTITY:
WITNESS WHO MATTERS:
EXPOSING EVENT:
SHAME OR GUILT MECHANISM:
PROTECTIVE TACTIC:
TRUTH CONCEALED:
REPAIR AVAILABLE:
COST OF REPAIR:
IDENTITY UPDATE:
```

## Diagnostic questions

- What does the character believe this event proves about them?
- Who must not see them this way?
- Are they protecting morality, belonging, status, or self-coherence?
- Do they want forgiveness, concealment, punishment, restitution, or control of the story?
- What would genuine repair require beyond words?

## Acceptance test

The mechanism passes when identity threat creates audience-specific behaviour, concealment has a clear purpose, guilt connects to a violated value, and repair carries consequence.
