# Perception and Observation

## Principle

Human writing does not report the whole room. It reveals what a particular person, under particular pressure, would notice.

Observation is selective. Selection exposes character.

A paramedic, a child, a thief, and a grieving son can enter the same kitchen and inhabit four different realities. The room is not the source of specificity. The observer is.

## Before writing

Determine:

- the viewpoint consciousness;
- their immediate objective;
- their bodily state;
- what they are trained to notice;
- what they are afraid of noticing;
- what detail might trigger memory or suspicion;
- what they misread because of bias, shame, desire, fatigue, or denial.

## Strong observation

A strong detail does at least two jobs. It may establish environment while revealing class, history, danger, relationship, or emotional pressure.

Prefer evidence over labels.

Weak: The apartment was neglected.

Stronger: Every lightbulb worked except the one above the rent notice.

The stronger detail is not automatically good because it is unusual. It works because it invites an inference and can plausibly be noticed.

## Sensory discipline

Do not cycle mechanically through sight, sound, smell, touch, and taste. Use the sense that carries dramatic information.

Sensory details must be:

- concrete enough to imagine;
- relevant to consciousness or pressure;
- proportionate to the scene;
- free of generic intensifiers;
- physically plausible.

## Common generated failures

- inventory description;
- interchangeable adjectives;
- cinematic camera language without viewpoint meaning;
- observations no person would make in the moment;
- every detail signalling theme too neatly;
- sensory details inserted on a checklist;
- poetic comparisons that belong to the authoring model rather than the character or narrative voice.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Why is this the detail selected?
2. Who would notice it?
3. What does it imply beyond itself?
4. Could the same sentence appear in fifty unrelated stories?
5. Is the detail discoverable through action rather than static description?
6. Does the observer avoid seeing something more important?

## Repair method

When prose feels generic:

1. remove summary labels;
2. return to viewpoint, objective, body, and bias;
3. list five plausible observations;
4. reject the most obvious three;
5. choose the detail with the strongest dramatic implication;
6. place it where attention would naturally land;
7. cut any explanation that repeats its meaning.

## Acceptance standard

The scene should contain at least one observation that feels inevitable for this consciousness and difficult to transfer unchanged to another story.
