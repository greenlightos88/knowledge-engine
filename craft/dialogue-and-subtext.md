# Dialogue and Subtext

## Dialogue is strategy

Dialogue is spoken behaviour used to alter another person, protect the self, control interpretation or avoid consequence. A line earns its place by doing something.

Common line actions include:

- recruit;
- test;
- conceal;
- provoke;
- reassure;
- dominate;
- bargain;
- distract;
- shame;
- seduce;
- refuse;
- confess;
- redefine.

## Subtext

Subtext is the active meaning beneath or beside the literal words. It emerges when a character cannot safely say what they want, does not fully understand it, or chooses an indirect tactic.

Subtext is not random ambiguity. The writer should know the concealed objective even when the audience does not.

## Voice model

Differentiate characters through:

- vocabulary and cultural register;
- sentence length and interruption tolerance;
- directness versus indirection;
- metaphor source;
- use of humour;
- comfort with silence;
- subjects they avoid;
- what they notice first;
- how status changes their speech;
- how stress degrades or sharpens their language.

A catchphrase is not a voice.

## Information discipline

People rarely tell each other what both already know unless the repetition has a tactical purpose. Exposition should be contested, incomplete, costly or behaviourally necessary.

Replace informational dialogue with:

- a disagreement over interpretation;
- a practical task requiring partial explanation;
- a lie exposed by specific knowledge;
- a status contest;
- a warning that reveals history through what remains unsaid;
- a consequence that makes the information self-evident.

## Compression

Strong dialogue often leaves connective tissue to performance and context. Remove greetings, confirmations, repeated names, emotional labels and restatements unless they create tension or character.

Compression does not mean every line is short. Rhythm should follow tactic, personality and pressure.

## Silence and interruption

Silence becomes meaningful when the audience understands what response was expected. Interruption becomes meaningful when it changes power, prevents disclosure, exposes eagerness or redirects attention.

## Dialogue diagnostic

For each exchange ask:

1. What does each speaker want from the other right now?
2. Why are they speaking instead of acting or leaving?
3. What can neither safely say?
4. How does each tactic change after resistance?
5. Which line changes the relationship or decision?
6. Could the voices be identified without character names?
7. What can be removed while preserving meaning?

## Anti-patterns

Reject:

- every character speaking with polished self-awareness;
- dialogue that summarizes the scene's emotional meaning;
- alternating complete speeches with no overlap or interruption;
- constant witty deflection;
- threats with no credible leverage;
- monologues that exist to announce theme;
- dialect used as ornament without cultural understanding;
- vague profundity replacing concrete conflict.

## Performance test

Read the scene aloud. Mark where actors would need to manufacture intention not present in the text. Repair the objective, obstacle or behaviour rather than adding parentheticals.
